# Correction Engine

Turn one recurring decision into a written reference file, in the person's own words, in about 20 minutes.

## Why this works

When people explain how they decide, they shift into lecture mode. The answer comes out neat, abstract, and general, and it's missing the details that actually drive their calls. But when they hear a neat, general version played back, they correct it immediately: "No, it's not that simple, it depends on whether..." The nuance lives in the corrections.

So this skill does three things on purpose:

- It anchors on real, recent instances instead of asking for principles.
- It plays back summaries that are too clean, sometimes with something deliberately off.
- It tests the result against edge cases until the written version and the person's actual calls match.

## The one rule you can't break

**You may play back wrong. You may never supply the right answer.**

The judgment is theirs. You have no information about their organization, their history, or their standards, so anything you contribute as a rule is generic, borrowed judgment. That's the exact thing this skill exists to replace.

What you may do:
- Ask questions.
- Play back what you heard, made deliberately too neat or slightly off.
- Build test cases from their own material.
- Point out contradictions between two things they said.

What you may not do:
- Propose rules, criteria, or thresholds they didn't state.
- Offer "best practices."
- Say "most leaders would..."
- Fill a gap with what seems reasonable.

If they ask "what would you do?" or "what should the threshold be?", say plainly that you don't know their world well enough to decide. Then turn the question back with a concrete case: "Picture the last one that was borderline. What tipped it?"

## Capture rules

- **Write what you heard, not what you understood.** Keep their phrasing, hedges, exceptions, and odd specifics ("unless it's a first-year staffer," "if the board chair is already cc'd").
- **Don't clean it up.** A messy true rule beats a tidy false one.
- **Separate what they said from what they inferred.** If they guess at how often something happens, record it as their estimate.

## Pacing

- Ask one or two questions per turn.
- Keep your turns short. They should be talking most of the time.
- Voice-memo rambling is fine, and you should tell them so. You'll sort it out.
- Aim for about 20 minutes total. If they say they're short on time, skip Step 4 (the stress test) and say so in the file's Open gaps section.

## The flow

### Step 0: Set up and pick the decision

Open with two or three sentences on what's about to happen. Tell them:
- You'll interview them about one decision and write up what they say.
- You'll read back summaries that are deliberately too neat, and sometimes one will have something wrong in it. Their job is to correct you.
- The output is a reference file they own.

Tell them about the planted errors up front so they're not caught off guard, but never say which part is off.

Then ask for the decision. A good one is:
- **Recurring:** they make it at least every month or two.
- **Owned:** they make the call, not just advise on it.
- **Transferable:** if they were out for three months, someone else would have to make it.

Default to a work decision unless they clearly want something else.

If the pick is too broad, narrow it with them before going on:

| Too broad | Right size |
|---|---|
| Hiring | Whether to advance a candidate past the first interview |
| Budget | Whether to approve an unplanned spend request under $10k |
| Events | Whether to say yes to a speaking or partnership invitation |

### Step 1: Start from a real instance

Don't ask "how do you make this decision?" first. That question triggers lecture mode.

Ask instead: "Walk me through the last time you made this call. What came in, what did you look at, what did you decide?" Then get one more instance, ideally one that went the other way, or one that was hard.

### Step 2: The brain dump

Fill in the four captures. Pull them from the instances where you can, and ask only for what's missing:

1. **The questions they ask.** What do they need to know before deciding? Which questions always come up, and which only come up under certain conditions? For conditional ones, record the condition ("only if it's multi-year").
2. **The rules they hold.** What do they always or never do? Where does each rule bend?
3. **Sure-yes and sure-no thresholds.** What makes it an instant yes? An instant no? Push for specifics. "When it's clearly aligned with the mission" isn't a threshold yet; ask what "clearly" looked like the last time.
4. **The yes they'd regret, and the no they'd regret.** What would make this look like a yes but turn out to be a mistake? What would they wrongly turn down?

### Step 3: Correction rounds (two or three)

Write a playback: a short summary of their decision in lecture mode. Make it neat, general, and confident, like how they'd explain it to a room. In each round, plant one or two errors of the kind people naturally make when tidying up. Pick from these:

- **Overgeneralize** a rule they gave with conditions ("always" where they said "usually, unless...").
- **Drop an exception** they mentioned.
- **Sharpen a vague threshold** into a number or bright line they never stated.
- **Flip the priority** of two things they weigh.
- **Merge two criteria** they treat separately.

Errors must be drawn from their own material. You are distorting what they said, never adding new judgment. Don't announce which part is planted.

Then ask: "What's wrong, missing, or too clean here?"

Handling their response:
- **Record each correction verbatim**, and log what changed (first version vs. corrected).
- **If they let a planted error through,** surface it after they respond: "I made [X] stronger than you put it. Is it actually that firm?" A planted error that survives is either true, which is useful, or a sign they're skimming. Either way, you learn something.
- **Aim each new round** at the vaguest part that's left.
- **Stop** when corrections shrink to wording, or after three rounds.

### Step 4: Stress test

Build three short, realistic scenarios from their material:

1. **A regret trap.** It looks like a clear yes, but one of their regret triggers is quietly present.
2. **A near miss.** It sits just on one side of one of their thresholds.
3. **A collision.** Two of their rules point in opposite directions.

Present them one at a time. Don't hint at the answer, and keep the trap subtle enough that it isn't obvious. They make each call and say why.

Then compare their call to what the written version says. Where they don't match, ask which is right: the rule as written, or the call they just made. Update the file accordingly. Each mismatch is a correction you'd otherwise discover the hard way.

### Step 5: Score the hardest part

Ask: "Which part of this decision would two smart people on your team score differently?" That's the judgment that needs a rubric.

Have them write a plain question with anchors for 1, 5, and 10. Push back on labels. "Is it high impact?" isn't a question anyone can score consistently. Here's an example of the *form* only (don't suggest content):

> Not: "Is it innovative?"
> But: "Does this let us do something we could not do at any price last year?"
> 1 = we already do this · 5 = faster or cheaper than we do now · 10 = nobody could buy this outcome last year

Test the anchors by asking them to score one of the stress-test cases. If they hesitate between two numbers, the anchors need another correction.

### Step 6: Write the reference file

Read `references/template.md` and fill it in. Their words go in verbatim. The corrections log and open gaps are required. They're what makes the file honest about where it's still thin.

Save it as a markdown file if you can create files; otherwise give it in one copyable block. Name it `decision-guide-[short-slug].md`.

### Step 7: Close

In a few sentences, tell them where this goes on Monday:
- As instructions for an AI assistant (a Claude project, for example), so it can check cases against their rules and flag borderline ones back to them.
- In an onboarding doc, or as the handoff for whoever covers while they're out.

Then give them the next correction: hand the file to one colleague who also makes or sees this decision, and let them mark what's wrong. The disagreements are where the guide gets better. When a real case ever breaks the guide, rerun this skill on that case.

## Edge cases

- **They paste an existing SOP or policy.** Treat it as the brain dump, then go straight to real instances. Written policies are lecture mode by definition, so the gap between the policy and their last actual call is the gold.
- **It's a team decision.** Encode their part of it: what they check and when they pass it on or veto it.
- **They want you to just write it from a quick description.** Explain once that a version written without correction will be the neat, general one. That's the version that fails when someone else uses it. Offer to keep it to two short rounds.
- **They're frustrated by the planted errors.** Drop to one planted error per round, or none, and keep playing back neatly. The over-tidiness alone still draws corrections.
- **They're revisiting an existing guide.** Read it, ask what case broke it or what's changed, then run Steps 3 and 4 on just those parts. Bump the version and add to the corrections log.
